What are they asking for?

The Life Sciences challenge asks how to tell whether brain cells are adapting, compensating for a problem or losing their ability to function.

A useful submission can show why the available measurements cannot answer a question, then propose the extra measurement that could. We can use this route with our existing simulation.

Our question: when the same brain pattern fits both preserved and reduced function, what extra measurement would tell them apart?

Read the official Life Sciences brief

What should we do next?

  1. Define the biology in plain language.

    Focus on SST cells, a group of neurons that help control the activity of other neurons. Define what useful function means in our proposed experiment. Explain how we would distinguish cells working harder to preserve it from cells failing to preserve it.

    Deliverable: one short research question, two possible explanations and a clear definition of function.

  2. Make the existing demonstration easy to reproduce.

    Package the full prototype with simple instructions. Show the same imaging inputs in both scenarios, the different assumed functional outputs, and how a new measurement can separate them. Show what changes when measurement noise increases.

    Deliverable: a working example that another person can run and check.

  3. Propose one real measurement.

    One proposed experiment is to measure how strongly identified SST cells inhibit other neurons before and during a defined stress. We would record both the response and how much SST activity is needed to maintain it.

    Compare the results with the starting level. Check that differences are not simply due to cell loss or inconsistent activation. This is a proposed biological test; we have not collected these data.

    Deliverable: a one page experiment plan with the measurement, controls and result that would change our conclusion.

  4. Show where our approach helps and where it fails.

    Compare repeating the scan, adding another score from it and measuring function independently. Include examples where extra noise, or similar functional outputs, leaves the two explanations unresolved.

    Deliverable: one clear comparison showing what each measurement can and cannot tell us.

  5. Test whether it helps researchers choose.

    Give people the same research question. Let one group use DopaTeam and another read a static report. Ask them to choose a measurement and explain why, using the same scoring rules.

    If we cannot run this test during the event, include the plan and make clear that the benefit remains to be tested.

    Deliverable: results showing whether the tool improves the decision, or a precise plan for testing this.

What should the final package contain?

  • The research question and why it matters.
  • The runnable code, setup instructions and test results.
  • A short explanation of the assumptions and the work completed during the hackathon.
  • The next experiment and what its result would tell us.
  • Sources for earlier research, reused code and any datasets.

Our calculations use simulated values. Published research provides context; biological validation and patient benefit have not been established.
